## Overview

Chapter 1 asks what contemplation (*hisbonenus*) really is. It sets surface study (*girsa*), which grasps a subject at first glance, against *iyun*, which stays with a concept until it reaches its depth, and pictures every concept as a river with width, length and depth. The recorded classes begin at §21. What people call deep concentration turns out to be only a vessel: the content comes from analysis, *bina sheb'bina*, held by *daas*. Delving (*ma'amik*) reaches the concept's root in the hidden depths of *chachma*, the aquifer under every spring. The chapter then maps the levels: *chachma sheb'bina*, new insight within one field; *bina sheb'chachma*, understanding one thing from another; and the order in which they grow from one another. It ends with *daas* itself: its length, width and depth, its focus on a single point, and its dependence on *bina*.

## Review questions

- Why is what people call deep concentration only a vessel, and what supplies the content that concentration works on? (§21)
- How does the analogy of springs and an aquifer explain *taalumos chachma*? (§24)
- What is a *tamtzis*, and how does learning to add illustrate it? (§25)
- How does *chachma sheb'bina* differ from *bina sheb'chachma*, as in the car engineer and the Wright brothers? (§31)
- Why must a student go through the length before he can reach the depth, and what goes wrong if he reaches for width first? (§37)
- Why do *daas* and *bina* depend on each other? (§48)
